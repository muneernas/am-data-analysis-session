Step 2 · Why this mattersPurpose

Data doesn’t replace teacher judgment — it sharpens it

  • 1See who is slipping before the next report card.
  • 2Check whether support and reteaching actually help.
  • 3Talk to heads of department and parents with evidence, not vibes.
  • 4Stop drowning in marksheets — start deciding.
For every subject

Math, English, Science, Arabic, PE, Arts — the method is the same. Only the columns change (skills, tasks, criteria, standards).

Step 2 · DefinitionCore idea

What is data analysis — really?

It is a disciplined habit: ask a useful question, look at evidence, find patterns, decide what to do.

Question→ Evidence→ Pattern→ Insight→ Action
Not just charts

A chart without a decision is decoration.

Not only for experts

If you can mark work, you can analyse patterns.

Ends in Monday

If nothing changes in class, you stopped too early.

Step 3 · MindsetHow we use data

Autopsy vs biopsy

Data as autopsy

  • ×Only after the unit ends
  • ×Focus on overall averages
  • ×Treat data as a reporting duty
  • ×Result: grades filed, little change

Data as biopsy

  • ✓During learning, while you can still help
  • ✓Focus on skills, tasks, groups
  • ✓Treat data as a diagnostic tool
  • ✓Result: teaching adjustments
Step 3 · MethodFour-step engine

Look → Patterns → Why → Action

01
Look

Open the data. Check averages and the spread. Notice outliers.

02
Find patterns

Which task, skill, group, or habit shows up again and again?

03
Ask why

Teaching method? Unclear task? Missing prior skills?

04
Take action

Reteach, regroup, or redesign the next lesson.

Stopping after Look is autopsy. The engine only works if you act.

Step 3 · Before any chartQuestions

Strong questions make strong analysis

Weak

  • ×“Show me all the scores.”
  • ×“Is the class okay?”
  • ×“Who failed?”

Strong

  • ✓“Which part of the unit was hardest for most students?”
  • ✓“Who improved from Term 1 to Term 2 — and who didn’t?”
  • ✓“Do students with low attendance also score lower?”
Move from “Who failed?” → “Which skill / task / habit is the problem?”
Step 3 · Look carefullyBeyond the average

Class average 75% is a starting point — not a story

1 · Spread

Are scores clustered near 75%, or split into two groups?

2 · High scores

Who already mastered it and needs a bigger challenge?

3 · Low scores

Who needs help this week — and on which part?

Rule: useful data is timely, easy to read, and ready to use in the next lesson.
Step 3 · ExampleSame average, different action

Worked example: average = 74%

Surface reading

“74%. We’re fine. Move on.”

Result

No change in teaching.

Pattern reading

“Math Term 1 for students under 80% attendance averages 58%.”

Result

Target those students + attendance check-ins.

Same overall number. Only the second reading creates a Monday move.

Step 3 · DiagnoseAsk why

Three places to look for the cause

Teaching

Was the method a poor match for the skill?

→ Try another approach or medium

The task

Was the question unclear or the rubric confusing?

→ Clarify the task next time

Basics

Did students miss earlier skills they needed?

→ Rebuild those basics first

Step 3 · ActThree teaching moves

Turn a pattern into a teaching move

Reteach

Whole-class gap → short, focused mini-lesson on one error pattern.

Regroup

Mixed understanding → flexible groups: support some, stretch others.

Redesign

Future units → change pacing, examples, or assessment design.

Insight formula: Who + What changed + By how much + So what

Step 3 · CautionCommon traps

Four traps that fool smart teachers

  • 1Average addiction — 74% can hide a group at 48%.
  • 2Link ≠ cause — robotics kids may score high for other reasons.
  • 3Cherry-picking — one chart that only supports what we hoped.
  • 4Missing context — a drop may mean a harder assessment.
Always ask: compared to what — and for whom?
